Task:
Write the possible_cause section for a diagnostic report.

Rules:
- Explain why the observed pattern might be happening.
- Do not repeat the anomaly_description. This section should explain
  likely meaning or cause, not restate the same signal list, exact
  values, risk level, or projection details already described in the
  previous section.
- Do not reclassify risk in this section. If you mention risk_level at
  all, it must exactly match the Risk Level shown in the input context.
  Prefer avoiding risk_level in possible_cause unless it is essential
  to explain uncertainty.
- Normal signal values do not change the Model Layer risk_level. Do
  not downgrade Medium or High risk to Low just because some key
  signals are within their normal ranges.
- For Low risk, keep this section short and focused. Usually 2 to 4
  sentences are enough: say the evidence is limited, give one or two
  plausible explanations, and avoid repeating the failure probability.
- For Low risk, start directly with possible explanations. Avoid
  generic opening sentences like "This may indicate a minor early
  variation..." when they do not add a concrete cause.
- Do not end Low-risk possible_cause with a repeated summary that says
  the risk is low and monitoring is recommended. That belongs in the
  description or action section.
- Do not close possible_cause with generic monitoring statements such
  as "this warrants monitoring" or "monitor to ensure it does not
  develop." End after explaining plausible reasons. Monitoring belongs
  in recommended_action.
- For Low risk, do not include exact numeric readings, risk-score
  percentages, or failure-projection percentages in possible_cause if
  they were already stated in anomaly_description. Refer to them in
  plain language instead, such as "a slightly elevated airflow reading"
  or "related readings are still normal."
- In all risk levels, avoid repeating exact numeric readings in
  possible_cause when anomaly_description already states them. Use
  plain references such as "current temperature readings" or "related
  readings" unless a number is essential to explain the cause.
- For Low risk, do not discuss the Failure Projection section in
  possible_cause unless it changes the cause explanation. Avoid
  repeating that the probability is very low; that belongs in
  anomaly_description.
- Do not exaggerate where a value sits within its reference range. If
  a value is within a wide normal range, do not describe it as near the
  upper or lower end unless the context clearly supports that wording.
- For dual-channel sensors, explain the concept in plain language
  before naming raw channels. Prefer "the two internal sensors inside
  the accelerator pedal" over leading with "Channel D and Channel E".
- Use retrieved fault knowledge to choose likely causes that match the component and key signals.
- Treat the input context as the main source of truth. If retrieved fault knowledge conflicts with the input context, follow the input context.
- Do not use a knowledge-base cause if the current input context does not support it.
- If retrieved fault knowledge is missing or says no specific knowledge was found, explain the cause from the input context only.
- Use careful wording such as "may indicate", "could suggest", or "could be related to".
- Do not say the cause is confirmed.
- Connect the possible cause to the key signal values in the context.
- If the signals are mixed or unclear, say the evidence is limited.
- Match certainty to risk_level. For Low risk, explain that the
  pattern is weak or early and may simply need monitoring unless more
  evidence appears.
- If the context includes a Failure Projection section, use only the
  failure probability or estimated cycles that are actually present.
  Do not invent missing probability, cycle count, dates, mileage, or
  deadlines.
- If estimated_failure_probability is present but
  estimated_cycles_to_failure is missing, do not say there is "no
  failure probability". Say no cycle estimate is available. If the
  probability is very small, describe it as a very low model-estimated
  probability of crossing the High-risk threshold, not as a calibrated
  probability of mechanical failure.
- Do not treat risk_score as a probability of mechanical failure.
  risk_score indicates current anomaly severity/classification, while
  estimated_failure_probability is the model-provided projection for
  crossing the High-risk threshold within the stated horizon.
- If estimated_failure_probability is very low and no cycle estimate
  is available, do not say the component "could fail soon" or is
  likely to fail soon.
- If the context includes Model Layer Notes that mention "forwarded
  from Data Layer proxy_decisions.csv" or "Data Layer proxy decision",
  you must explain that the likely cause assessment is based partly on
  rule-based proxy evidence forwarded by the Data Layer, not only on
  TTM residual scoring.
- Do not show internal source filenames such as "proxy_decisions.csv"
  in owner-facing report text. Say "rule-based Data Layer evidence" or
  "a rule-based diagnostic flag" instead.
- Do not show raw diagnostic trouble codes such as P0113 or P0106 in
  owner-facing report text. Translate them into plain component
  language, such as "a diagnostic flag related to the intake air
  temperature sensor." Exact codes belong in a technical-details UI,
  not in this cause explanation.
- If the context includes a Detection Provenance section, do not omit
  it. Use it to explain why a high-risk proxy-forwarded case can exist
  even when some current readings are normal.
- If proxy-forwarded risk is High but the displayed key signals are
  NORMAL, state that the evidence may reflect an intermittent or
  decision-level diagnostic pattern. Do not claim the current
  readings alone prove a mechanical fault.
- Only say current readings are normal when every displayed key signal
  is marked NORMAL. If any key signal is ABNORMAL, acknowledge the
  abnormal signal and do not use wording such as "current readings
  still look normal."
- If the context includes Model Layer Notes, use them only to explain
  provenance or data-quality limits when helpful. Do not treat notes
  as possible mechanical causes. Do not copy Model Layer Notes or
  retrieved source text verbatim into the report.
- Follow certainty guidance for wording strength, but never say the system has confirmed a real fault.
- Do not name a specific mechanical failure mode unless it matches
  the direction of the key signals. For example, do not suggest a
  closed-thermostat failure mode for a lower-than-expected coolant
  temperature pattern unless overheating evidence is present.
- For lower-than-expected coolant temperature, slow warm-up, or
  falling coolant temperature without high-temperature evidence, limit
  the possible-cause explanation to cautious possibilities such as
  sensor reading issue, cooling fan behavior, or insufficient evidence
  for a specific cause. Avoid explaining thermostat mechanics for this
  low-risk pattern.
- If audience is "non-technical vehicle owner", use plain language for a vehicle owner without automotive expertise.
- Avoid unexplained technical jargon.
- If a technical term is necessary, explain it in simple words.
- Avoid pipeline or model-window wording such as "this window." For a
  vehicle owner, say "current readings" or "during this short driving
  period" only if timing is necessary; otherwise omit the phrase.
- For proxy-forwarded cases with NORMAL displayed key signals, keep
  the cause explanation to verification-level possibilities such as
  intermittent sensor signal, loose connector, corrosion, or early
  sensor drift. Do not say the pattern will evolve into a confirmed
  fault.
- If the context does not support a possible cause, write a clear placeholder explanation instead of "N/A".

Examples:

Bad example (accelerator_pedal_sensor, High risk):
"Pedal D/E mismatch. Accelerator sensor fault or wiring issue."

Why this is wrong:
- Uses raw field names (TPS, accel_pedal_d) and unexplained acronyms
  without explaining what they mean
- Lists causes without explanation or context
- Telegraphic style not suitable for non-technical audience
- Does not connect causes to the observed signal pattern
- Does not use hedging language (presents as definite causes)

Good example (accelerator_pedal_sensor, High risk):
"This pattern may indicate an issue with the accelerator pedal
position sensor. Modern cars usually compare two pedal sensor
channels for safety. In this case, the two pedal readings do not
track each other as closely as expected, which could be related to a
sensor-channel issue, a wiring or connector problem, or early sensor
wear. The data suggests the car may not be getting a fully consistent
signal about how far the pedal is being pressed."

Why this works:
- Uses hedging language throughout ("may indicate", "could include")
- Explains why the two pedal channels are compared
- Connects causes to the observed pedal-channel mismatch
- Uses plain language to explain the system behavior
- Helps owner understand why inconsistent pedal readings matter

Bad example (accelerator_pedal_sensor, Low risk):
"Dual pedal sensor channels show divergence. Sensor fault, wiring
corrosion, or connector issue confirmed."

Why this is wrong:
- Uses technical jargon (dual pedal sensor channels, divergence)
- Claims fault is confirmed when it's only a prediction
- Lists causes without explaining what they mean
- Does not acknowledge the low risk level or normal signal ranges

Good example (accelerator_pedal_sensor, Low risk):
"This could suggest a small difference between the two accelerator
pedal sensor channels. Modern cars use two separate sensor channels
in the pedal for safety checking. The readings show a minor
variation between the two channels, which might be related to normal
sensor tolerance, a slight wiring connection issue, or early signs of
sensor wear. This does not strongly point to a confirmed sensor fault
at this stage."

Why this works:
- Uses appropriate hedging for low-confidence scenario ("could
  suggest", "might be related to")
- Explains why cars have dual sensors (safety checking)
- Acknowledges uncertainty without reclassifying risk
- Provides context that helps owner understand this may be normal
  variation

Bad example (accelerator_pedal_sensor, Medium risk):
"Since both channel readings are within their normal ranges and the
overall risk level is Low, this pattern does not strongly confirm a
fault but warrants monitoring."

Why this is wrong:
- Changes the risk level from the input context
- Repeats normal signal evidence from anomaly_description
- Ends with a generic monitoring summary instead of explaining causes

Good example (accelerator_pedal_sensor, Medium risk):
"Possible explanations include early sensor wear, a small wiring or
connector issue, or normal tolerance between the two pedal sensor
channels. The current evidence does not confirm a fault, but it
explains why this sensor should be checked if the pattern continues."

Why this works:
- Does not repeat exact channel values
- Does not reclassify the risk level
- Goes directly to plausible explanations

Bad example (air_intake_maf_anomaly, Low risk):

0.0-123.0 g/s. Other signals are normal. The failure probability is

Why this is wrong:
- Repeats exact numbers already covered in anomaly_description
- Repeats projection details instead of explaining a possible cause

Good example (air_intake_maf_anomaly, Low risk):

Why this works:
- Explains plausible causes without repeating exact signal values
- Starts directly with possible explanations
- Uses plain language for a vehicle owner
- Keeps the wording appropriately cautious for Low risk
- Does not repeat failure-projection details
